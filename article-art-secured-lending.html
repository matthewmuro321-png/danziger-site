Borrowing against art

Art is valuable, illiquid, mobile, and easy to sell out from under a lender. That combination is what makes lending against it unlike any other secured loan.

Art Law and Finance · 2022

A great collection is a great deal of value sitting on a wall, doing nothing for its owner's balance sheet. Borrowing against art lets a collector, dealer, or estate unlock liquidity without selling, which is its appeal, and a growing number of private banks and specialist lenders compete to provide it. But art is among the most difficult assets to lend against, because it has a set of qualities that ordinary collateral does not, and a lender who treats a painting like a piece of equipment will discover the difference at the worst time. Understanding why art is hard collateral is the key to structuring a loan that holds.

Art is unusual collateral

Four qualities set art apart. It is illiquid, so realizing value on a default can take months and a sale process, not a phone call. Its value is volatile and subjective, dependent on shifting taste and on appraisals that are estimates rather than quotes, which is why lenders advance conservatively, often around half of appraised value, and require professional valuation. It is mobile, able to cross a state or national border in the back of a van, which raises questions about where a security interest is perfected and whether it remains so after the work moves. And it is exposed to title and authenticity risk, because a work that turns out to be stolen, encumbered, or fake is collateral worth nothing, which means a careful lender runs the same provenance, title, and authenticity diligence on the collateral that a careful buyer runs before a purchase.

The lender's central problem: control and priority

The deepest difficulty is keeping the lien attached to the work. A lender takes a security interest in the art and perfects it by filing under Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code, which is straightforward enough. The problem is that two well-established rules can cut the lien off entirely. Under the buyer-in-ordinary-course rule, a buyer who purchases from a dealer in the ordinary course of the dealer's business can take the work free of a security interest the dealer created. And under the entrustment rule, entrusting goods to a merchant who deals in goods of that kind gives the merchant the power to transfer good title to such a buyer. Put together, if the collateral sits with a dealer, the lender's perfected lien can be extinguished by a sale the lender never sees coming.

Even where the borrower is a private collector rather than a dealer, the risk is that the collector quietly sells or consigns the work. The lender's nightmare is the same in every version: the collateral walks, a buyer takes it clean, and the lender is left with a filing that points at a painting now hanging in someone else's home.

Why lenders care so much about the work itself

In most secured lending, perfection by filing is enough. In art lending it may not be, because the buyer-in-ordinary-course and entrustment rules can defeat a filed lien. That is why control of the work, and control of the chain through which it could be sold, is often as important to an art lender as the loan terms.

Possession, or the wall

Lenders answer the control problem in one of two ways, and the choice defines the loan.

 Possessory loanNon-possessory loan
Where the work isWith the lender or an independent custodianOn the borrower's wall
Control and sale riskStrong; the work cannot be sold out from under the lenderWeaker; relies on filings, covenants, and trust
Cost to the borrowerLoses the use and display of the workKeeps living with the art
Typical termsHigher advance rates, broader lender comfortLower advance rates, stronger borrowers, more diligence

Illustrative. Many lenders require possession or a controlled custody arrangement; private banks may lend against art a client keeps at home, at lower leverage and to borrowers whose other assets provide comfort.

A possessory structure, in which the lender or a neutral custodian holds the work, gives the lender control and largely defeats the sale and entrustment risk, at the cost of taking the art off the borrower's wall. A non-possessory structure lets the collector keep living with the work, which is often the entire point of borrowing rather than selling, but it leaves the lender more exposed and so usually comes with lower leverage, more diligence, and reliance on the borrower's overall creditworthiness.

The rest of the package

Around the core structure sits the work that makes an art loan sound. The lender conducts title, provenance, and authenticity diligence on the collateral itself, because a defect in the work is a defect in the security. It requires insurance, typically assigned to the lender, and addresses storage, condition, and environmental controls. It plans for the work's mobility, since moving the art to another state or country can raise re-perfection and choice-of-law questions that must be managed rather than discovered. And it documents the deal through a loan agreement and a security agreement, the UCC filings, any custody or control arrangement, the appraisal, and the insurance assignment, with attention to whether the loan is recourse or non-recourse to the borrower's other assets.

For dealers borrowing against inventory, the entrustment and ordinary-course problems are at their most acute, since the dealer's entire business is selling the very collateral the lender is relying on. Inventory financing in the art trade therefore depends on tight controls, sale procedures that route proceeds to the lender, and ongoing verification that the works are where they are supposed to be.
