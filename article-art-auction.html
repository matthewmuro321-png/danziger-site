Selling an important work at auction

The major auction houses present a standard consignment agreement. For an important piece, almost none of it is actually fixed.

Art Law · 2020

When a collector or an estate decides to sell a significant work at auction, the house presents a printed consignment agreement that has the air of a form to be signed rather than negotiated. For an ordinary lot, it largely is. For an important work, that impression is misleading and expensive, because the desirability of a major piece is leverage, and much of the agreement moves once the house wants the consignment. Knowing which terms move, and which carry exposure that survives the sale, is the difference between a strong result and a costly one.

Estimate and reserve

Two numbers frame the sale. The estimate, published as a low and a high, sets the market's expectations, and an estimate pitched too high can chill bidding while one too low can leave money on the table. The reserve is the confidential minimum below which the work will not sell, and by convention it cannot exceed the low estimate. Setting both deliberately is the first negotiation, because they govern both how the work is marketed and the floor of protection against an underwhelming night.

The economics: commission, premium, and the overage

The headline economics are more negotiable than they appear. The seller's commission, the vendor's commission charged to the consignor, is frequently reduced for important works, sometimes to zero, and on truly desirable pieces the consignor may negotiate enhanced terms, sharing in the buyer's premium or receiving a portion of the proceeds above a threshold. The buyer's premium is set by the house and paid by the buyer, but for a major consignment the consignor can negotiate a share of it. These are not fixed costs of doing business; they are the price of winning the consignment, and the house pays it for works it wants.

Guarantees and irrevocable bids

For important works, the question of who bears the risk that the piece underperforms is answered by a guarantee. A house guarantee commits the auction house to pay the consignor a minimum amount regardless of whether the work sells, transferring the downside to the house. A third-party guarantee, often executed as an irrevocable bid, brings in an outside party who commits to bid a minimum, taking the risk off the house in exchange for a financing fee and a share of any upside. Either way, the guaranteed amount, the way the overage above it is split, and the fee paid for the guarantee are central negotiated terms, and on a significant piece they can matter as much as the commission.

The exposure that outlives the sale

The consignor warrants title, authenticity, and provenance, and the house in turn guarantees authenticity to the buyer. If a buyer later rescinds for an authenticity problem, that liability flows back to the consignor, who may have to return the proceeds years after the sale. Negotiate the scope and survival of those warranties; this is real, deferred risk.

The terms that protect you

Beyond the economics, a cluster of operational terms determines whether the consignor is genuinely protected. The house's marketing and cataloguing commitments, the illustration, the catalogue placement, any touring or exhibition, and the marketing spend, drive the result and should be specified rather than left to the house's discretion. Insurance and risk of loss while the work is in the house's possession must be allocated clearly. And the settlement terms deserve close attention, including when the consignor is paid after the sale and, critically, the consignor's recourse if the buyer defaults on payment, since a consignor wants to know whether it is paid regardless or only when the house actually collects.

Finally, the consignor should address the right to withdraw the work and the fees for doing so, the confidentiality of the arrangement, and what happens if the work goes unsold, including any post-sale private treaty efforts and the terms of a buy-in. On a major consignment, each of these is a point of leverage, and the consignor who negotiates them holds far more of the value than the one who signs the form.

Terms that move for an important lot

01

Estimate and reserve. The published expectations and the confidential floor.

02

Seller's commission and enhanced terms. Often reduced to zero or better for desirable works.

03

Buyer's premium sharing. A negotiated share of the premium the buyer pays.

04

Guarantee and overage split. The guaranteed amount, the fee, and how upside is shared.

05

Warranty scope and survival. The title and authenticity exposure that can return years later.

06

Settlement and buyer default. When you are paid, and your recourse if the buyer does not pay.

07

Marketing commitments. Catalogue placement, touring, and spend, made specific.

08

Withdrawal and the unsold scenario. Your rights and costs if you pull the work or it fails to sell.
