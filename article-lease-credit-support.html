Standing behind the lease

A commercial lease is a multi-year credit decision. Before the rent is settled, the landlord negotiates the security behind the tenant's promise, and the tenant negotiates to give as little of it as it safely can.

Real Estate and Leasing · 2021

When a landlord signs a commercial lease, it is extending years of occupancy on the strength of a promise to pay. That makes a lease, beneath the square footage and the rent schedule, a credit decision, and like any credit decision it turns on two questions: how well the lender knows the borrower, and what stands behind the obligation if the borrower fails. In leasing, those questions are answered by financial diligence on the tenant and by the credit support, the security deposit, the guaranty, and the letter of credit, that backstops the rent. After the economics themselves, this is the most negotiated part of the lease, because each form of security allocates the risk of a tenant's failure in a different way.

Knowing the counterparty

Before a landlord can set the right security, it has to understand who it is dealing with, and the financial diligence comes first. A landlord assesses the tenant's creditworthiness through financial statements, tax returns, bank references, third-party credit reports, the tenant's operating history, and, where a guaranty is offered, the net worth and liquidity of the proposed guarantor. The output of that diligence sets the price of the credit support. An established, well-capitalized tenant, all the more so a public or investment-grade one, can command light security or none, because its balance sheet is the security. A startup, a newly formed entity, or a thinly capitalized operating company commands far more.

The landlord's recurring problem is the tenant that signs as a single-purpose entity with few assets of its own, an LLC formed to hold one lease. If that entity fails, there is nothing behind it, which is precisely why landlords look past the entity to a deposit, a guaranty from someone with assets, or a bank's letter of credit. The weaker the tenant on paper, the more the lease leans on these external supports.

The security deposit

The most familiar form of security is cash. The tenant deposits a sum, commonly measured in months of rent, that the landlord may apply against unpaid rent or other defaults, returning the balance at the end of the term. The negotiation runs to the amount, to whether the deposit burns down over time as the tenant establishes a payment record, and to the conditions for its return. In New York, a landlord holding a security deposit must keep it in trust and may not commingle it with its own funds, a protection that matters most precisely when a landlord is in financial trouble.

A cash deposit is simple, but it has limits from both sides. For the tenant, it is capital tied up rather than deployed in the business. For the landlord, it is finite, it is only as large as was negotiated, and, as discussed below, it can become entangled in a tenant's bankruptcy at the very moment the landlord needs it.

The guaranty

A guaranty brings a third party behind the lease. A parent company, an affiliate, or an individual principal promises to perform the tenant's obligations if the tenant does not, which is valuable exactly to the extent the guarantor is more creditworthy than the tenant. The negotiation turns on who guarantees, a deep-pocketed parent being worth far more than an individual of modest means, on the scope of the guaranty, whether it is a full and unconditional guaranty of payment and performance or a limited one, and on its duration, whether it runs for the whole term or burns off on conditions.

The good guy guaranty

In New York commercial leasing, one limited guaranty is so common it has a name. Under a good guy guaranty, an individual principal personally guarantees the tenant's obligations only up to the point at which the tenant actually vacates and surrenders the premises, broom-clean and on proper notice, with the rent paid through that date. The guarantor is not on the hook for the rent that would otherwise accrue across the rest of the term; the personal exposure is capped at the obligations that build up while the tenant remains in possession.

The structure is a sensible bargain for both sides. It gives the landlord a creditworthy individual standing behind the rent and, just as important, a powerful incentive for the tenant to leave promptly rather than hold over in a failing business, since the guarantor's personal liability keeps growing until the keys are returned. It gives the principal a defined, bounded personal risk rather than an open-ended one. The points that get negotiated are the conditions that make the guaranty burn off: the required notice, the surrender of vacant and clean possession, and payment of all rent through the surrender date. A good guy guaranty is a middle ground, and in the New York market it is often the expected one.

The letter of credit

Increasingly, landlords prefer that the security take the form of an irrevocable standby letter of credit, in which the tenant's bank, rather than the tenant, stands behind the obligation. The landlord draws on the letter of credit if the tenant defaults, and the bank pays. The reason sophisticated landlords favor it over cash becomes clear at the moment of greatest stress, the tenant's insolvency, and turns on a point of law worth understanding.

A letter of credit is the independent obligation of the issuing bank, not the tenant's money and not property of the tenant's bankruptcy estate. A cash security deposit, by contrast, is applied against the landlord's claim against the tenant, and that claim is capped by the Bankruptcy Code, which limits a landlord's damages from a terminated lease to roughly the greater of one year's rent or a portion of the remaining term. Because a letter of credit is the bank's separate promise, a draw on it generally falls outside the automatic stay that freezes action against a bankrupt tenant, and courts have divided on whether and how the statutory cap limits what a landlord may ultimately keep from the draw. That uncertainty cuts in the landlord's favor, which is exactly why landlords like letters of credit: they may deliver value that a capped, estate-bound cash deposit cannot.

Why the form matters most when it matters most

All three supports look similar while the tenant is paying. They diverge in bankruptcy. A cash deposit is tied to a capped claim against the estate; a guaranty and a letter of credit are obligations of someone other than the bankrupt tenant, and so can reach value the cap and the estate would otherwise put out of reach.

A letter of credit carries its own negotiated terms. The landlord cares about the amount, the creditworthiness of the issuing bank, an evergreen clause that renews the instrument automatically and lets the landlord draw if it is not renewed, and transferability so the letter can pass to a buyer when the building is sold. The tenant cares about cost, because a letter of credit ties up its borrowing capacity and usually requires it to post collateral with the bank, and so negotiates to reduce or burn down the amount as it performs. The draw conditions, ideally a simple sight draft rather than a documentary obstacle course, are negotiated too, since a letter of credit the landlord cannot easily draw on is worth less than it appears.

The forms, compared

The three supports are not substitutes so much as different allocations of risk and cost, and landlords often combine them, a deposit or letter of credit alongside a good guy guaranty, with the mix set by the diligence.

The negotiation, in the round

For a landlord, the amount and form of security is a price term like any other, traded against the rent, the free-rent period, and the tenant-improvement allowance, and informed throughout by what the diligence revealed about the tenant. For a tenant, the goal is to give credit support that is adequate but not excessive, to prefer the form that costs it least, and to build in reductions that reward a record of paying on time. The result that serves both sides is one that matches the security to the genuine risk: enough to make the landlord whole if the tenant fails, structured so that it actually performs when that failure arrives, and no heavier on the tenant than the risk requires.
