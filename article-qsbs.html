QSBS and the entity question

The exclusion can make millions of dollars of gain entirely tax-free. The most common structuring mistake, an LLC that elects to be taxed as a C-corp, forfeits all of it.

Startups and Venture Capital · 2025

Few provisions in the tax code reward founders and early investors as richly as the qualified small business stock exclusion. Get the structure right, hold the stock long enough, and a large slice of the gain on a sale can escape federal tax entirely. Get the structure wrong, and the benefit simply is not there, no matter how successful the company becomes. The frustrating part is that one of the most common ways to get it wrong looks, to a founder, like getting it right. Setting up a Delaware limited liability company and electing to have it taxed as a C corporation feels like it should qualify. It does not. Understanding why is worth a great deal of money.

What the exclusion does, and the recent upgrade

Section 1202 of the tax code allows a taxpayer to exclude from federal income tax the gain on the sale of qualified small business stock, subject to limits. The benefit is capped at the greater of a dollar figure or ten times the taxpayer's basis in the stock, applied per company. For founders and early employees and investors who experience a large exit, the result can be millions of dollars of gain that are never taxed at the federal level.

The regime was significantly expanded in 2025. For stock issued after July 4, 2025, the law introduced a tiered exclusion, allowing 50 percent of the gain to be excluded after a three-year hold, 75 percent after four years, and 100 percent after five years, where previously a full five-year hold was required for any exclusion at all. It raised the per-company cap from $10 million to $15 million, and it lifted the ceiling on a company's gross assets from $50 million to $75 million, both indexed for inflation going forward. Stock issued on or before that date remains under the older rules, with the five-year hold and the $10 million cap. The practical effect is that the exclusion is now larger, available sooner in partial form, and open to a wider set of companies.

The requirements, and who is left out

The exclusion comes with conditions, and they are exacting. The stock must be stock in a domestic C corporation. It must be acquired at original issuance, meaning from the company itself rather than bought from another holder. The company's aggregate gross assets must have been at or below the ceiling, now $75 million, at the time the stock was issued. The company must be engaged in an active qualified business, and here many businesses fall out, because the rules exclude a long list of activities, including most professional services, finance, farming, and the hospitality and restaurant trades. And the stock must be held for the required period.

That exclusion list matters for how a firm advises across industries. The qualified small business stock benefit is built for product and technology companies, not for service or hospitality businesses, so a restaurant group or a hotel company is generally outside it regardless of structure. For the venture-backed company building a product, however, it is one of the most valuable tax attributes a founder can have, which is exactly why the entity decision deserves care.

Why an LLC taxed as a C-corp does not qualify

Here is the trap. A limited liability company can elect, by checking a box, to be taxed as a C corporation. Founders often hear that qualified small business stock requires a C corporation, make that election, and believe they have positioned the company for the exclusion. They have not. The election changes how the entity is taxed; it does not change what the entity is. For purposes of the exclusion, the law requires stock in a corporation, and an LLC is not a corporation under state law and does not issue stock. It issues membership interests. An LLC with a C corporation tax election is therefore the worst of both worlds for this purpose: it is taxed as a corporation, losing the pass-through treatment that is an LLC's main advantage, while still failing to qualify for the exclusion that a real corporation would provide.

The misconception, stated plainly

Electing to have an LLC taxed as a C corporation does not make it qualified small business stock. The exclusion requires actual stock in an actual corporation. The tax election gives a founder corporate-style taxation and none of the qualified small business stock benefit.

Getting the benefit, and the timing that matters

There are two ways to actually capture the exclusion. The first is to incorporate as a C corporation from the outset, typically a Delaware C corporation, which starts the holding-period clock at the earliest possible moment and is also the form venture investors expect. The second is to begin as an LLC and later convert it into a C corporation, through a statutory conversion or by contributing the business to a new corporation.

The conversion path preserves the early flexibility of an LLC, including the ability to pass losses through to the founders while the company is young, but it carries two timing consequences a founder must understand. The holding-period clock for the exclusion starts at the conversion, not when the LLC was formed, so the years spent as an LLC do not count toward the three, four, or five years required. And the gross-assets test is applied at conversion, which means a company that waits to convert until it has grown large can find it has crossed the $75 million ceiling and lost eligibility entirely. The lesson is that if the exclusion is part of the plan, converting earlier is almost always safer than converting later.

Choosing at formation

The decision comes down to a few honest questions. A company that will raise venture capital, that wants the exclusion clock running as early as possible, and that does not need to pass early losses to its owners is usually best served by incorporating as a Delaware C corporation at the start. A company that expects meaningful early losses its founders could use, or that genuinely benefits from pass-through flexibility in its first phase, may reasonably begin as an LLC and convert before it raises institutional money or grows past the asset ceiling. What almost no company should do, if the exclusion matters to it, is elect to have an LLC taxed as a C corporation and assume the benefit follows. It does not, and discovering that at the moment of a sale is an expensive way to learn it.
