Plan before the windfall

The most valuable estate planning around a business sale happens before the sale, not after. Once the deal closes and the cash arrives, the best moves are already gone.

Family Office and Private Wealth · 2023

For most founders, nearly all of their wealth lives inside one illiquid asset: the company they built. A sale, an initial public offering, or a recapitalization converts that asset into liquid wealth, and it is a moment of enormous opportunity. It is also, for estate-planning purposes, a door that swings shut. The instant the price is set and the proceeds land, a large fortune crystallizes in the owner's taxable estate at a known, high value, and the most powerful planning techniques, the ones that move future appreciation to the next generation, become unavailable. The wealthiest outcomes belong to the families who plan while the value is still uncertain and still low, not after it has been fixed by a closing.

Why before beats after

The logic is about appreciation. When an owner gives away an interest in the company before a sale, the gift removes not only that interest's current value from the estate but also all of its future growth, including the premium a buyer is about to pay. The same gift made after the sale is a gift of cash at full value, with no future appreciation left to shift and no room left to maneuver. Worse for the after case and better for the before case, a minority, non-controlling interest in a private company can often be valued at a discount for its lack of control and marketability, so a pre-sale gift can move more economic value while using less of the owner's lifetime exemption. The leverage lives entirely in the timing.

The ammunition is now permanent

What makes these moves possible is the lifetime gift and estate tax exemption, the amount an individual can transfer free of transfer tax, which now stands at $15 million per person and $30 million for a married couple and, following recent legislation, is permanent rather than scheduled to fall. That capacity is most valuable when it is applied to an asset poised to multiply in value, which is exactly the situation of a company approaching a sale. Using the exemption to move pre-sale equity is using a fixed amount of shelter to protect a far larger future fortune.

The pre-liquidity toolkit

The techniques share a single goal, to shift the company's future appreciation out of the taxable estate before the sale locks it in, and they range from simple to sophisticated.

The principal vehicles

01

Gifts to irrevocable trusts. Moving equity to a dynasty or generation-skipping trust for the family removes it, and its growth, from the estate.

02

Grantor retained annuity trusts. A GRAT transfers appreciation above a set rate to heirs with little or no gift tax, well suited to an asset about to spike.

03

Spousal lifetime access trusts. A SLAT uses the exemption to fund a trust for a spouse, shifting value while preserving indirect access to it.

04

Sales to grantor trusts. Selling the appreciating interest to a grantor trust for a note freezes today's value in the estate and moves the growth out.

05

Valuation discounts. Gifting a minority interest before a sale can support discounts that stretch the exemption further.

06

QSBS multiplication and charitable trusts. Where the stock qualifies for the small business stock exclusion, distributing it to multiple trusts can multiply the exclusion; a charitable remainder trust can defer the gain and add an income stream.

Timing is everything, and unforgiving

These techniques work only if they are done early, and rushing them at the closing table is where families get hurt. If equity is transferred after a sale has become a near-certainty, with a binding agreement or a deal so far advanced that the gain is effectively locked in, the tax authorities can invoke the anticipatory assignment of income doctrine and tax the resulting gain to the original owner anyway, erasing the benefit the family was reaching for. Valuation discounts evaporate as a sale approaches and the market sets a price. And a gift must be a completed transfer of the asset itself, not a transfer of the proceeds after the fact. The practical consequence is that serious pre-liquidity planning begins before there is a signed letter of intent, ideally while a sale is still merely a possibility on the horizon.

The toothpaste rule

You cannot put it back. Once the deal is signed and the wealth is fixed and liquid, the appreciation is already in your estate and the discounts are gone. Every month of advance planning before that point is worth more than any amount of effort after it.

The New York overlay

For New York families there is an added reason to start early. New York imposes its own estate tax with a far lower exemption and a punishing cliff, and it applies a three-year clawback that pulls gifts made within three years of death back into the taxable estate. That clawback means a gift made too close to the end does not achieve its New York purpose, so the planning must be done not only before the sale but well before, and for many families it pairs naturally with a hard look at whether to change domicile away from New York altogether.

The discipline

The families who capture the most from a liquidity event treat the estate plan as part of the deal preparation, not an afterthought to it. They begin while a sale is still hypothetical, coordinate the estate planning with the transaction team so the two do not work at cross purposes, and weigh honestly the real cost of these techniques, which is giving up some measure of control over and access to the wealth being shifted. Done with time to spare, the planning can move an extraordinary amount of value to the next generation at little or no transfer-tax cost. Done in the final weeks before a closing, it is a missed opportunity dressed up as a plan.
