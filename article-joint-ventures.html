Negotiating a joint venture

A joint venture is a marriage of capital and capability with no default divorce law. Control, money, and exit are whatever the agreement says they are, which is exactly why the agreement is the whole game.

Corporate and M&A · 2021

A joint venture pairs two parties who each have something the other needs. One brings capital, the other brings expertise, a market position, technology, or assets, and together they build something neither could build alone. The appeal is obvious. The difficulty is that, unlike a public-company merger, a joint venture comes with very little useful default law to fill the gaps, so almost everything that will eventually matter has to be written down in advance. The agreement is the venture's constitution, and the negotiation is really an argument about three questions: who controls it, who profits from it, and how anyone gets out. The headline ownership split, the number everyone fixates on, is usually the least important answer of the three.

Contributions and what they are worth

The first negotiation is over what each side puts in and what it is credited for. Cash is easy to value. Everything else is not. When one party contributes assets, intellectual property, a brand, or services, the question of how to value that non-cash contribution is a frequent and consequential flashpoint, because that valuation drives the initial ownership split. A party that talks its contribution up gets more of the venture for it.

More important than the opening split is what happens when the venture needs more money, which it almost always will. The agreement must address future capital: whether members can be required to fund additional calls, what happens to a member who will not or cannot fund, and the consequences of that failure. Those consequences, ranging from simple dilution to punitive dilution to member loans that accrue against the defaulting party, are where a minority or less-liquid partner can quietly lose much of its position over time. The capital-call mechanics deserve as much attention as the initial split, because they determine whether the split survives.

Control and the reserved matters

Control in a joint venture is not a single lever; it is a division of decisions. Day-to-day operating decisions usually sit with a managing member or a management team, while a defined list of fundamental decisions requires a higher vote, a supermajority, unanimity, or a specific minority consent. That list, often called the reserved or protective matters, is the true seat of control, and negotiating it is the heart of the deal. It typically covers the budget, taking on debt, issuing new equity or calling capital, selling the business or major assets, related-party transactions, hiring or firing key people, and changing the nature of the business.

The tension is structural. The capital partner wants control proportional to the money it has put at risk. The operating partner wants protection against being overridden and autonomy to run the business it knows how to run. A well-negotiated reserved-matters list resolves that tension deliberately, giving the operator room to operate while protecting each side against the decisions that could impair its investment. A poorly negotiated one leaves a minority member exposed on exactly the decisions that matter most.

The number that matters least

Parties negotiate the equity split hardest and the mechanics around it least, which is backwards. A 50% stake with weak protective rights, punishing capital calls, and an unfavorable deadlock mechanism is worth far less than a smaller stake with the mechanics drafted in your favor. The split is the headline; the mechanics are the deal.

Economics and the waterfall

How the venture shares money is a separate question from who owns it, and conflating the two is a common error. Distributions can be made pro rata to ownership, but in many ventures they follow a waterfall instead: a return of capital first, then a preferred return or hurdle to the capital partner, and then a promote, a disproportionate share of the upside paid to the operating partner as a reward for performance. Management fees may sit on top, and the agreement should provide for tax distributions so members are not taxed on income they have not received in cash. The equity percentages and the distribution waterfall are different instruments, and a sophisticated party negotiates both, because the waterfall can shift far more value than the split.

Deadlock

When two partners share control and cannot agree, the venture can freeze, and the agreement should say in advance how the freeze is broken. Mild mechanisms escalate the dispute to senior principals or to mediation. When those fail, the break-glass remedies take over: a buy-sell, in which one party names a price and the other must either buy or sell at it, a put or call right, or dissolution of the venture. The choice among these is not neutral. A buy-sell, for example, quietly favors the party with the deeper pockets, because it is the one able to fund a buyout when the trigger is pulled. Negotiating the deadlock mechanism is negotiating who wins when the partnership stops working.

Transfer and exit

Exit is the most undernegotiated and most consequential part of many joint ventures, because the parties are focused on building and not on parting. The agreement should restrict transfers of interests, typically through rights of first refusal or first offer, and address tag-along and drag-along rights, which protect a minority that wants to join a sale and a majority that wants to compel one. It should define the exit paths, whether by buy-sell, a sale of the venture, a public offering, or paired put and call rights, and the events that trigger them, such as the passage of time, performance milestones, a change of control of a member, or a default. And it must specify how an exiting member's interest is valued, by appraisal or formula, because a clean exit right with an unclear price is not clean at all.

Duties, defaults, and protections

Around this core sit the terms that govern conduct and breach. The agreement should set default remedies for failures to fund or perform, address whether members owe one another fiduciary duties and to what extent those duties are modified or waived, which varies by the law chosen to govern the entity, and handle non-competition and exclusivity, confidentiality, the ownership of intellectual property the venture develops, indemnification, and dispute resolution. Members who are competitors in their own businesses care intensely about the scope of exclusivity and about who owns what the venture creates, and those terms are worth negotiating with the same care as the economics.

Where the leverage actually sits

01

Capital calls and dilution. Whether you can be forced to fund, and what happens to your stake if you do not.

02

Reserved matters. The list of decisions that need your consent. This is control.

03

The distribution waterfall. Preferred returns, hurdles, and the promote, which move more value than the split.

04

The deadlock mechanism. Who wins when the partners cannot agree, often the party with more capital.

05

Transfer, tag, and drag. Who can sell, who can be dragged, and who can come along.

06

Exit triggers and valuation. How and when you get out, and at what price.

The asymmetry to watch

The two sides of a joint venture want different protections, and they do not want them equally. The capital partner cares most about return of and on its money, about control over the decisions that affect value, and about a clean exit. The operating partner cares most about autonomy, about its promote, and about not being diluted or overridden out of the upside it is creating. The party that is more sophisticated, or simply has more leverage, tends to win the mechanics even when the headline split looks even, because it knows that the capital calls, the waterfall, the reserved matters, and the deadlock and exit provisions are where the real power lives. The lesson for anyone entering a joint venture is to negotiate those mechanics as if they decide the outcome, because they do.
