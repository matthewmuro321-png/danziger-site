Four ways to grow a restaurant

Company-owned, joint venture, license, and franchise are a single spectrum, trading control and upside against capital and speed. And one legal trap runs through all of them.

Hospitality, Franchise, and Corporate · 2022

A restaurant concept that works faces a good problem: how to open more of it. The answer is rarely just one thing, because there are four basic ways to grow and finance a restaurant brand, and they sit on a spectrum. At one end is the company-owned unit, where the brand keeps total control and takes all the risk and all the upside. At the other is the franchise, where the brand puts in almost no capital, grows quickly, and gives up much of the control. Between them lie the joint venture and the license. Choosing among them is one of the defining decisions a growing brand makes, because each carries its own economics, its own control, its own risk, and its own body of law. The figures below are typical ranges; every deal is its own negotiation.

Model oneThe company-owned unit

In a company-owned model, the brand entity does everything. It signs the lease, funds the build-out, employs the staff, runs the kitchen, and keeps every dollar of profit. This is the purest expression of a concept and the way most restaurants begin. Its advantage is total control: the brand can guarantee consistency, capture all the upside, and protect the experience without negotiating with anyone. Its cost is capital and risk, because the company funds each unit and bears each unit's downside, which makes this the slowest and most capital-intensive way to grow.

Financing a company-owned unit usually blends equity and debt. The owners contribute equity, often raising it from investors through an entity formed for the location or the group, which brings securities law into play whenever passive investors are involved. Debt comes from bank or small-business loans and equipment financing, and the landlord typically contributes a tenant improvement allowance toward the build-out. The legal work centers on the entity structure, frequently a separate company per location to contain risk, the lease, the liquor license, employment, and the investor and financing documents.

Typical economicsBuild-out commonly runs from a few hundred thousand dollars for a small fast-casual space to several million for a full-service or flagship location. The brand keeps 100% of the profit but funds 100% of the cost. Restaurant-level margins are thin, with prime cost, food plus labor, often consuming roughly 55% to 65% of sales and unit-level operating margins commonly in the low double digits.

Model twoThe joint venture

A joint venture brings in a partner to share the capital, and often the local operating expertise, of opening units. The brand and the partner co-own a venture entity, contribute capital in agreed proportions, and split the profits according to an operating agreement. The partner may be a financial investor providing capital, a seasoned local operator providing market knowledge and management, or both. The appeal is that a brand can grow faster than it could alone while retaining real involvement and a meaningful share of the upside, sitting between the total control of a company unit and the hands-off economics of a franchise.

The legal heart of a joint venture is the operating agreement, which governs the capital contributions, the profit and loss splits, distributions, management and control, deadlock, transfer restrictions, buy-sell rights, non-competition, and exit. Alongside it, the brand licenses its trademarks and system into the venture so the venture may operate under the name, and the brand often takes a management role to protect standards. Where outside investors are passive, securities law applies, and the members owe one another fiduciary duties. A well-drafted joint venture is mostly a well-drafted operating agreement.

Typical economicsThe brand commonly takes an equity stake in exchange for its concept, its know-how, and some capital, often in the range of 20% to 50%, with the capital partner funding the majority. The brand frequently also earns a management fee, commonly around 3% to 6% of revenue, and may charge a brand royalty within the venture. Capital partners often negotiate a preferred return before profits are split. Distributions then follow ownership percentages.

Model threeThe license

A license grants someone the right to use the brand, and sometimes the concept and recipes, for a fee, without the comprehensive system, support, and control that define a franchise. Licensing is how a restaurant brand ends up inside a hotel, an airport, or a stadium, how a celebrity chef lends a name to an operator, and how many concepts expand internationally, through a local licensee who runs the business under the brand. It is capital-light for the brand and lighter-touch than a franchise, which is its appeal and, as explained below, its central danger.

The defining legal issue of a license is that it can accidentally be a franchise. The law does not care what the parties call their deal; it looks at three elements, and the franchise trap is discussed in its own section below. To stay a license rather than a franchise, the arrangement must be structured so that it lacks one of those elements or fits a recognized exemption, which usually means the licensor does not exercise the comprehensive control or provide the comprehensive assistance that a franchisor does. That restraint sits in tension with trademark law, which requires a brand owner to exercise quality control over its mark or risk losing it, so a license must thread a needle: enough control to protect the mark, not so much that it becomes a franchise.

Typical economicsLicense royalties commonly run in the range of roughly 2% to 8% of revenue, sometimes structured instead as a flat fee or a profit share, frequently with an upfront license fee. Hotel and celebrity-chef licenses vary widely and are often bespoke. The brand deploys little or no capital and provides correspondingly less support than a franchisor would.

Model fourThe franchise

In a franchise, the franchisor grants a franchisee the right to operate a unit under the brand and the franchisor's entire business system, from recipes and operations to training, marketing, and standards, in exchange for fees. The franchisee owns and funds the unit and runs it as an independent business; the franchisor supplies the system and ongoing support. This is the classic capital-light expansion model, and it lets a brand scale far faster than it could on its own balance sheet, financed largely by its franchisees.

It is also the most heavily regulated of the four. Federal law, through the FTC Franchise Rule, requires the franchisor to give every prospective franchisee a Franchise Disclosure Document, a detailed 23-item disclosure, at least 14 days before the franchisee signs or pays. On top of that, roughly a dozen states, New York among them, require the franchisor to register the disclosure document before offering or selling franchises there, and New York's Franchise Act adds its own anti-fraud and registration regime. Some states further regulate the ongoing relationship, constraining termination, renewal, and transfer. The documents are the disclosure document, the franchise agreement itself, and, for multi-unit deals, an area development agreement.

Typical economicsThe initial franchise fee per unit is commonly in the range of about $25,000 to $50,000. The ongoing royalty is typically around 4% to 6% of gross sales, with a separate advertising or marketing fund contribution commonly around 1% to 4%. Terms usually run about 10 years with renewal options. The franchisee funds the entire build-out, and multi-unit deals carry additional area development fees.

The legal thread: the franchise trap

One issue runs through this entire spectrum and deserves singular attention, because it catches brands that never intended to franchise. Under federal and state law, a relationship is a franchise, no matter what the parties call it, if it has three elements: the operator uses the brand's trademark, the brand exercises significant control over or provides significant assistance to the operator's method of operation, and the operator pays a required fee. Put all three together and you have a franchise, with all the disclosure and registration obligations that follow, even if your agreement is titled a license or a joint venture.

The consequences of getting this wrong are serious. An inadvertent, unregistered franchise can expose the brand to rescission, damages, and regulatory penalties, and in a registration state like New York the exposure is real. That is why a license must be structured deliberately, to lack one of the three elements or to fit a recognized exemption, and why even a joint venture or an area arrangement should be reviewed against the franchise definition. The discipline is to decide, on purpose, whether you are franchising, and then to build the relationship to match that decision rather than stumbling across the line by accident.

Practice note

If a deal has a trademark, a fee, and meaningful control or support, assume it may be a franchise until you have confirmed otherwise. The cheapest moment to address the franchise laws is while structuring the relationship; the most expensive is after an operator has invoked them.

Choosing, and mixing

No single structure is best, and the strongest brands use several at once. They keep flagship and signature locations company-owned to protect the experience and capture the full upside, grow into key markets through joint ventures with capital and operating partners, place outlets in hotels, airports, and foreign markets by license, and scale broadly by franchise where the concept is systematized enough to run from a manual. The right mix follows from the brand's capital, its tolerance for ceding control, its growth ambition, and how systematized and protectable the concept is. What matters is that each structure is chosen deliberately and built correctly, because the difference between them is more than economics. It determines who controls the brand, who bears the risk, and which body of law governs the relationship.
