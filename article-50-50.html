50:50 Partnerships and LLCs

Issues, drafting problems, practical suggestions, and sample clauses for equal owner business arrangements

Corporate & Securities · 2019

Key Takeaways

A 50:50 ownership split solves economics, but it does not solve governance.

The agreement should separate ordinary course authority from reserved matters requiring both owners approval.

Deadlock provisions should provide a business solution before litigation becomes the only solution.

Funding, transfers, buyouts, valuation, member services, information rights, and related party transactions should be drafted in detail.

Dissolution should usually be a last resort unless the parties deliberately want a quick exit right.

Introduction

Equal ownership is common in closely held businesses. Two founders may each contribute capital and services. Two investors may acquire property together. Two professionals may form a firm. Two family members may inherit or acquire a business on equal terms. In each case, the parties often want the documents to reflect a simple understanding: each side owns one half and each side has an equal voice.

That simplicity is appealing, but it is also dangerous. A 50:50 structure creates equality of economics and equality of control. It also creates the possibility that no one can make a final decision. If the governing agreement does not address decision making, deadlock, funding, transfers, buyouts, and exit rights, the parties may end up with a business that cannot act and owners who cannot separate cleanly.

The better approach is not to avoid equal ownership in every case. The better approach is to draft for it. A well drafted 50:50 agreement assumes that trust may decline, liquidity needs may diverge, business judgment may differ, and one owner may eventually want out. The goal is not to make disagreement impossible. The goal is to make disagreement manageable.

A 50:50 agreement should protect the relationship while it works and protect the business if the relationship breaks down.

1. The Core Problem: Equal Ownership Does Not Mean Functional Governance

A 50:50 arrangement answers the economic question but often fails to answer the governance question. The economic answer is straightforward: each owner receives 50% of profits, losses, distributions, and value, subject to the agreement. The governance answer is harder: who decides when the owners disagree?

If both owners must approve everything, the business may become paralyzed. If either owner can act alone, the business may be exposed to unauthorized contracts, excessive spending, inconsistent strategy, and unilateral commitments. If one owner is the day to day operator and the other is passive, equal ownership may not reflect equal labor. If both owners are active, the agreement needs to allocate authority clearly enough that employees, lenders, landlords, vendors, accountants, and customers know who can speak for the company.

Suggestions

Do not rely on a general statement that the members manage the company equally.

Identify who can sign contracts, approve expenses, hire employees, borrow money, enter leases, make tax elections, settle disputes, and call capital.

Divide decisions into ordinary course matters, reserved matters, and emergency matters.

Use an annual budget to define ordinary course authority.

If one owner is the operator, give that owner practical authority but reserve material decisions for both owners.

Sample Clause: Management Structure

Management of the Company. The Company shall be managed by the Members acting through a management committee consisting of one representative appointed by each Member. Each Member shall have one vote on the management committee. Except for Ordinary Course Matters delegated under this Agreement and Reserved Matters requiring unanimous approval, decisions of the management committee shall require the approval of both Members.

Designated Officers. The Members may appoint officers or authorized representatives to perform specified functions for the Company. The authority of each officer or representative shall be limited to the authority expressly granted by the Members or set forth in the approved annual budget. No officer or representative shall have authority to bind the Company outside the ordinary course of business unless approved in accordance with this Agreement.

No Implied Authority. Except as expressly provided in this Agreement, no Member shall have authority to bind the Company, incur indebtedness, execute contracts, employ or terminate senior personnel, make capital expenditures, confess judgment, settle claims, transfer assets, or take action outside the ordinary course without the approval required under this Agreement.

2. Ordinary Course Decisions and Reserved Matters

One of the most important drafting tasks is to separate ordinary course decisions from major decisions. Without this distinction, either the business becomes inefficient because every decision requires both owners approval, or the non operating owner is exposed because the operating owner can act too freely.

Ordinary course matters are routine decisions consistent with the approved budget and business plan. They may include purchasing inventory, paying approved expenses, collecting receivables, managing employees below a stated level, ordinary marketing, customer service, maintenance, and routine vendor contracts.

Reserved matters are decisions that materially affect ownership, control, capital, liability, strategy, or value. They should require approval of both owners.

Common Reserved Matters

Admission of a new member or partner.

Issuance of additional equity or options.

Amendment of the operating agreement or partnership agreement.

Sale of all or substantially all assets.

Merger, conversion, consolidation, or reorganization.

Borrowing above a stated threshold.

Guaranteeing obligations.

Capital expenditures above a stated threshold.

Approval of the annual budget.

Entering into or amending leases.

Affiliate or related party transactions.

Hiring, firing, or changing compensation of senior employees.

Changing the company business.

Making tax elections.

Settling litigation above a stated amount.

Filing bankruptcy.

Dissolving or winding up the company.

Approving capital calls.

Making non routine distributions.

Changing accountants, counsel, or banks.

Suggestions

Use dollar thresholds rather than vague materiality standards whenever possible.

Tie ordinary course authority to the approved budget.

Address what happens if the annual budget is not approved.

State whether the prior year budget continues, and whether it may increase by a fixed percentage for inflation or required costs.

Make emergency authority narrow and require prompt notice to the other owner.

Sample Clause: Reserved Matters

Reserved Matters. Notwithstanding anything to the contrary in this Agreement, the Company shall not take any of the following actions without the prior written approval of both Members: amend governing documents; admit a new Member; issue equity or equity rights; sell all or substantially all assets; merge, convert, reorganize, dissolve, or liquidate; incur debt or grant liens outside the approved budget; make capital expenditures above stated thresholds; approve or amend the annual budget; enter into material contracts; enter into related party transactions; hire or terminate senior personnel; settle material claims; make material tax elections; require additional capital contributions; make non routine distributions; or take any action outside the ordinary course of business.

3. Deadlock: The Defining Risk in a 50:50 Entity

Deadlock is the central risk of equal ownership. A deadlock occurs when the owners cannot approve a decision that requires approval of both. What matters is not whether the owners disagree but whether the disagreement prevents the business from operating in accordance with its governing documents.

In New York LLCs, judicial dissolution may be available when it is not reasonably practicable to carry on the business in conformity with the articles of organization or operating agreement. That standard makes the operating agreement central. A court often needs to know what the business was supposed to be, how it was supposed to operate, and whether it can still function under the agreed structure.

Common Drafting Mistakes

The agreement says major decisions require both owners approval but does not say what happens if approval is not obtained.

The agreement defines deadlock too broadly, allowing one owner to manufacture deadlock over minor issues.

The agreement defines deadlock too narrowly, leaving the parties stuck unless the dispute involves a small list of matters.

The agreement requires mediation but provides no remedy if mediation fails.

The agreement allows dissolution too quickly, giving one party leverage to threaten value destruction.

The agreement prohibits dissolution without providing a meaningful contractual exit.

Suggestions

Define deadlock carefully and require written notice.

Require at least one or two meetings before the dispute becomes a formal deadlock.

Include a status quo clause requiring the business to continue in the ordinary course.

Choose the deadlock remedy based on the business type.

Use dissolution as a final remedy unless the parties deliberately want an easy exit.

Sample Clause: Balanced Deadlock Procedure

Deadlock. A Deadlock shall exist if the Members are unable to approve a Reserved Matter after two duly called meetings held at least ten business days apart, provided that the Member asserting Deadlock has delivered written notice describing the disputed Reserved Matter in reasonable detail and explaining why the failure to approve such matter materially impairs the Company business or the Members rights under this Agreement.

Executive Meetings. Upon a Deadlock, the Members shall meet in person or by videoconference at least twice within fifteen business days after delivery of the Deadlock notice. Each Member shall participate personally and in good faith.

Mediation. If the Deadlock is not resolved through executive meetings, either Member may submit the dispute to nonbinding mediation before a mediator experienced in closely held business disputes. The mediation shall occur within thirty days after demand unless the Members agree otherwise.

Status Quo. During the Deadlock process, the Company shall continue to operate in the ordinary course consistent with the most recently approved annual budget. No Member shall take unilateral action outside the ordinary course, withdraw funds, terminate material contracts, divert business, interfere with employees, or impair Company assets.

Failure of Resolution. If the Deadlock is not resolved within sixty days after the initial Deadlock notice, either Member may initiate the buy sell procedure set forth in this Agreement.

4. Choosing the Right Deadlock Remedy

Deadlock remedies should fit the business and the bargaining position of the parties. There is no universally correct mechanism.

Common Remedies

Mediation is useful as an early step but weak as the only remedy.

Expert determination works well for narrow technical disputes, such as accounting treatment, reserve levels, or whether a capital expenditure is necessary.

Rotating authority may work where decisions fall into separate categories, such as operations and finance.

An independent tie breaker can work where the tie breaker has industry knowledge and a limited mandate.

A buy sell provision often provides the most effective final remedy because it turns deadlock into separation.

A third party sale process may be appropriate for real estate assets or investment vehicles.

Judicial dissolution may be appropriate as a final remedy, but it is expensive and uncertain.

Suggestions

Use a tiered process: meeting, mediation, buy sell, then dissolution only if necessary.

Do not allow a buy sell immediately after a trivial dispute.

Do not require endless procedures before an owner can exit.

For unequal liquidity situations, consider appraisal based buyout rather than a shotgun.

For real estate entities, consider a sale process or partition style mechanism.

For operating companies, consider allowing the operator to buy out the passive owner at appraised value, subject to protections against opportunistic conduct.

5. Shotgun Clauses

A shotgun clause allows one owner to name a price and terms. The recipient must either sell at that price or buy the initiating owner interest at that same price and terms. The theory is that the initiating owner will name a fair price because it does not know whether it will be buyer or seller.

The problem is liquidity. If one owner has more cash or better financing, that owner can name a low price knowing the other may be unable to buy. Shotgun clauses can be efficient among equally sophisticated and similarly capitalized parties, but unfair where there is a liquidity imbalance.

Suggestions

Require a deadlock or other trigger before the shotgun can be used.

Require disclosure of material company information before the offer.

Prohibit a defaulting owner from initiating the shotgun.

Require cash terms unless both parties agree otherwise.

Provide enough time to arrange financing.

Include closing mechanics, representations, releases, and default remedies.

Consider excluding shotgun rights during the first year or two.

Sample Clause: Shotgun Buy Sell

Shotgun Right. If a Deadlock remains unresolved for sixty days after delivery of a Deadlock notice, either Member may deliver a written notice to the other Member stating a cash valuation for one hundred percent of the equity of the Company and the material terms upon which the initiating Member is willing either to purchase the other Member entire Membership Interest or sell the initiating Member entire Membership Interest.

Election. Within thirty days after receipt of the Shotgun Notice, the receiving Member shall elect either to sell its entire Membership Interest to the initiating Member or to purchase the initiating Member entire Membership Interest, in each case at the valuation and on the terms stated in the Shotgun Notice.

Failure to Elect. If the receiving Member fails to make a timely election, the receiving Member shall be deemed to have elected to sell its Membership Interest to the initiating Member.

Closing. Closing shall occur within sixty days after the election or deemed election. The purchase price shall be paid in immediately available funds at closing unless the Shotgun Notice expressly provides more favorable payment terms.

Information Condition. No Member may deliver a Shotgun Notice unless such Member has first provided the other Member with reasonable access to current financial statements, bank records, material contracts, debt documents, tax returns, and other material Company information reasonably necessary to evaluate the Company.

Default. If the purchasing Member fails to close, the selling Member may elect to purchase the defaulting Member Membership Interest at a ten percent discount to the valuation stated in the Shotgun Notice, seek specific performance, recover damages, or pursue any other remedy available under this Agreement or applicable law.

6. Texas Shootout and Sealed Bid Procedures

A Texas shootout requires each owner to submit a sealed bid. The higher bidder purchases the lower bidder interest, usually at the higher bidder price. This can produce a market based result between the owners, but it also requires both parties to have financing capacity and risk tolerance.

Suggestions

Use this only for sophisticated parties.

Require evidence of funds or financing.

Set a clear bid submission process.

Use neutral counsel, an escrow agent, or accountant to hold bids.

Address tie bids.

Address failure to close.

Decide whether the winning bid price applies to the seller 50% interest or implies a total company valuation.

Sample Clause: Sealed Bid Buyout

Sealed Bid Procedure. If a Deadlock remains unresolved for sixty days, either Member may initiate a sealed bid procedure by written notice. Within fifteen business days after such notice, each Member shall submit to the Company outside counsel a sealed bid stating the cash purchase price that such Member is willing to pay for the other Member entire Membership Interest.

Opening of Bids. The bids shall be opened simultaneously by Company counsel on the first business day after the bid deadline. The Member submitting the higher bid shall purchase the other Member Membership Interest at the price stated in the higher bid.

Tie Bid. If the bids are identical, the Members shall submit a second round of sealed bids within five business days. If the second round bids are identical, the purchasing Member shall be selected by lot.

Closing. Closing shall occur within sixty days after the bids are opened. The purchase price shall be paid in cash at closing.

Failure to Close. If the winning bidder fails to close, the nondefaulting Member may purchase the defaulting Member Membership Interest at the nondefaulting Member bid price, seek specific performance, recover damages, or pursue any other remedy available under this Agreement.

7. Appraisal Based Buyouts

An appraisal based buyout may be fairer where one party has more liquidity or more company information. Instead of one party naming a price, one or more appraisers determine value. The downside is cost, delay, and valuation disputes. The agreement must specify the valuation standard.

Valuation Issues to Address

Fair market value or fair value.

Going concern value or liquidation value.

Valuation date.

Treatment of debt.

Treatment of owner compensation.

Treatment of pending litigation.

Treatment of tax liabilities.

Treatment of non operating assets.

Treatment of goodwill.

Whether to apply minority discounts.

Whether to apply marketability discounts.

Whether to apply discounts for cause.

Whether the purchase price is paid in cash or over time.

Whether deferred payments are secured.

Suggestions

For a 50% owner buyout, consider prohibiting minority and marketability discounts unless the seller is in default.

For a misconduct buyout, consider a discount, but make sure it is reasonable and enforceable.

For deferred payments, require a promissory note, interest, security interest, pledge of the purchased interest, and acceleration on default.

For real estate entities, consider using appraisers with MAI credentials.

For operating businesses, require appraisers with relevant industry experience.

Sample Clause: Appraisal Based Valuation

Fair Market Value. Fair Market Value shall mean the price at which the applicable Membership Interest would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under compulsion to buy or sell and both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts, assuming the Company continues as a going concern.

No Discounts. Unless the buyout arises from a Member Default, fraud, misappropriation, or Cause Event, no minority discount, lack of control discount, or lack of marketability discount shall apply to the valuation of a fifty percent Membership Interest.

Appraisers. If the Members do not agree on Fair Market Value within fifteen business days, each Member shall select one qualified appraiser within ten business days. The two appraisers shall select a third appraiser. If either Member fails to select an appraiser, the appraiser selected by the other Member shall determine Fair Market Value.

Determination. If the two appraisals differ by ten percent or less, Fair Market Value shall be the average of the two appraisals. If the two appraisals differ by more than ten percent, the third appraiser shall determine Fair Market Value, provided that the third appraiser determination shall be within the range established by the first two appraisals.

Costs. Each Member shall pay the fees of its own appraiser, and the Company shall pay the fees of the third appraiser unless the appraisers determine that one Member acted in bad faith, in which case such Member shall pay the third appraiser fees.

8. Capital Contributions and Capital Calls

Funding disputes are common in 50:50 entities. One owner may want to grow aggressively. The other may want to preserve cash. One may be able to contribute. The other may not. If the agreement does not address funding, a capital need can become a control dispute.

Issues to Address

Initial capital contributions.

Whether capital accounts must remain equal.

Whether services count as capital.

Whether future contributions are mandatory or optional.

Who can approve a capital call.

What happens if one owner does not contribute.

Whether the contributing owner receives a loan, preferred return, or dilution.

Whether failure to fund is a default.

Whether emergency funding is permitted.

Whether third party debt is preferred before member contributions.

Suggestions

Do not allow unilateral capital calls except for narrow emergency or budgeted items.

Decide whether non contribution causes dilution.

Consider treating excess contributions as member loans before dilution.

For real estate entities, address lender required capital.

For operating businesses, address working capital shortfalls.

For service businesses, decide whether sweat equity creates ongoing contribution obligations.

Sample Clause: Capital Calls

Initial Contributions. Each Member has made or shall make the initial capital contribution set forth on Schedule A. Except as expressly provided in this Agreement, no Member shall be required to make additional capital contributions.

Required Capital Calls. Additional capital contributions shall require approval of both Members, except that the Managing Member may require contributions necessary to pay taxes, insurance, payroll, debt service, rent, emergency repairs, or other obligations expressly included in the approved annual budget.

Notice. A capital call notice shall state the amount required, the purpose of the contribution, each Member share, the due date, and whether failure to contribute will result in a member loan, default, dilution, or other remedy.

Failure to Contribute. If a Member fails to make a required contribution within ten business days after notice, the contributing Member may elect to fund the shortfall as a loan to the Company. Such loan shall bear interest at the prime rate plus four percent and shall be repaid before discretionary distributions are made to the Members.

Dilution. No Member percentage interest shall be diluted for failure to contribute unless the capital call notice expressly states that dilution may result and the capital call was approved in accordance with this Agreement.

9. Distributions and Reserves

Equal ownership does not guarantee equal liquidity needs. One owner may want distributions. The other may prefer reinvestment. The agreement should distinguish tax distributions from discretionary distributions.

Suggestions

Provide for tax distributions if the entity is taxed as a partnership.

Require reasonable reserves for debt service, taxes, working capital, repairs, insurance, litigation, and budgeted expenses.

Specify whether distributions require both owners approval.

Prohibit non pro rata distributions unless expressly approved.

Address whether member loans must be repaid before distributions.

Sample Clause: Distributions

Tax Distributions. Subject to applicable law, lender restrictions, and available cash, the Company shall make tax distributions to the Members in amounts intended to enable each Member to pay federal, state, and local income taxes attributable to such Member allocable share of Company taxable income. Tax distributions shall be treated as advances against other distributions.

Discretionary Distributions. Additional distributions shall be made only upon approval of both Members after taking into account reasonable reserves for operating expenses, debt service, taxes, insurance, capital expenditures, legal compliance, working capital, and contingent liabilities.

No Preferential Distributions. No distribution shall be made to one Member unless a proportionate distribution is made to the other Member, except for repayment of approved member loans or as otherwise expressly provided in this Agreement.

10. Compensation, Employment, and Services

Many 50:50 disputes are not really about ownership. They are about labor. One owner may work full time while the other contributes capital only. One may manage operations while the other manages relationships. One may stop working but still expect 50% of distributions.

The agreement should separate ownership rights from employment or service rights.

Issues to Address

Required time commitment.

Specific duties.

Salary or guaranteed payments.

Bonuses.

Expense reimbursement.

Benefits.

Authority to hire staff.

Removal from position.

Effect of termination on ownership.

Disability.

Retirement.

Nonperformance.

Outside business activities.

Suggestions

Do not assume equal ownership means no salary.

If one owner works materially more than the other, salary or guaranteed payments may be appropriate.

Use schedules to list responsibilities.

Require periodic review of compensation.

State whether termination of employment triggers a buyout.

Distinguish termination for cause from resignation without cause.

Sample Clause: Member Services

Duties. Each Member shall perform the duties assigned to such Member on Schedule B. Each Member shall devote such time, attention, and skill to the Company as is reasonably necessary to perform such duties.

Compensation. No Member shall receive salary, guaranteed payments, consulting fees, management fees, bonuses, or other compensation from the Company except as expressly approved in the annual budget or by written approval of both Members.

Expenses. Each Member shall be reimbursed for reasonable business expenses incurred on behalf of the Company, provided that such expenses are documented and consistent with the approved budget or otherwise approved in accordance with this Agreement.

Termination of Services. Termination of a Member employment, consulting relationship, or service role shall not, by itself, terminate such Member ownership interest unless this Agreement expressly provides for a buyout upon such termination.

11. Books, Records, Reporting, and Bank Controls

Information rights are essential in 50:50 entities. A non operating owner should not have to litigate to obtain bank statements or contracts. An operating owner should not be burdened by constant, disruptive information demands.

Suggestions

Require monthly financial reporting.

Give both owners online view access to bank accounts.

Require dual signatures above a threshold.

Require annual tax returns to be delivered before filing.

Require use of a recognized accounting system.

Prohibit cash withdrawals without documentation.

Require separate company accounts.

Require approval before changing banks or accountants.

Sample Clause: Books and Records

Books and Records. The Company shall maintain complete and accurate books and records in accordance with commercially reasonable accounting practices. The books and records shall include bank statements, financial statements, tax returns, contracts, invoices, payroll records, loan documents, insurance policies, leases, licenses, permits, and material correspondence.

Access. Each Member shall have full and equal access to the Company books and records during normal business hours upon reasonable notice. The Company shall provide each Member with online view access to all Company bank accounts and accounting records to the extent reasonably available.

Monthly Reporting. Within twenty days after the end of each month, the Company shall provide each Member with a profit and loss statement, balance sheet, cash balance report, accounts receivable report, accounts payable report, and bank reconciliation.

Tax Returns. Draft tax returns shall be provided to each Member at least fifteen business days before filing. The Company shall not file any material tax return or make any material tax election without the approval required under this Agreement.

12. Related Party Transactions

Affiliate transactions often create suspicion. A member may lease property to the company, provide services through an affiliate, hire family members, borrow from the company, or direct business to another entity. These transactions should not be prohibited in every case, but they should be disclosed and approved.

Suggestions

Require full written disclosure.

Require approval by the disinterested owner.

Use market terms.

Document the transaction.

Provide audit rights.

Make unauthorized related party transactions voidable.

Sample Clause: Related Party Transactions

Related Party Transactions. The Company shall not enter into any transaction, agreement, lease, loan, compensation arrangement, reimbursement arrangement, service arrangement, or other business relationship with any Member or affiliate of a Member unless the transaction is fully disclosed in writing and approved in advance by the disinterested Member.

Market Terms. Any approved related party transaction shall be on terms no less favorable to the Company than those that would be available in an arm length transaction with an unaffiliated third party.

Voidable Transaction. Any related party transaction entered into without the approval required under this Section shall be voidable at the election of the disinterested Member, without limiting any other remedy available to the Company or such Member.

13. Fiduciary Duties, Exculpation, and Standards of Conduct

Fiduciary duty issues are jurisdiction specific and should be handled carefully. Many LLC statutes allow operating agreements to define or modify duties to some extent, but there are limits. A broad waiver may not be appropriate in every deal, and it may not be enforceable in every context.

Suggestions

State the expected standard of conduct.

Define permitted outside activities.

Address business opportunities.

Include confidentiality obligations.

Include exculpation for ordinary negligence or good faith business judgment if appropriate.

Carve out fraud, bad faith, willful misconduct, knowing violation of law, and misappropriation.

Do not use a broad waiver without considering enforceability.

Sample Clause: Standard of Conduct and Exculpation

Standard of Conduct. Each Member shall act in good faith and in a manner such Member reasonably believes to be in or not opposed to the best interests of the Company. Each Member shall refrain from fraud, willful misconduct, bad faith, knowing violation of law, gross negligence, and misappropriation of Company assets or opportunities.

Exculpation. No Member shall be liable to the Company or the other Member for monetary damages arising from actions taken in good faith and within the scope of such Member authority, except to the extent such damages result from fraud, willful misconduct, bad faith, knowing violation of law, gross negligence, misappropriation, unauthorized self dealing, or breach of this Agreement.

Outside Activities. A Member may engage in outside business activities that do not materially interfere with such Member duties to the Company and do not involve misuse of Company confidential information, Company assets, or Company opportunities.

14. Business Opportunities and Competition

Two equal owners may disagree about whether an opportunity belongs to the company. This is especially important in real estate, investment, consulting, brokerage, development, technology, and professional service businesses.

Suggestions

Define the company line of business.

Define excluded opportunities.

Require disclosure of opportunities within the company business.

Create a rejection process.

Allow outside opportunities only after disclosure and rejection.

Address passive investments separately.

Sample Clause: Business Opportunities

Company Opportunities. No Member shall directly or indirectly pursue, acquire, or profit from any business opportunity within the Company approved line of business unless such opportunity has first been disclosed to the Company in reasonable detail and the Company has declined to pursue such opportunity.

Rejection. The Company shall be deemed to have declined an opportunity if both Members agree in writing not to pursue it or if the Company fails to approve pursuit of the opportunity within fifteen business days after disclosure.

Excluded Opportunities. The restrictions in this Section shall not apply to passive investments representing less than five percent of a class of publicly traded securities or to opportunities listed on Schedule C.

15. Transfers and Ownership Changes

In a 50:50 business, each owner chose the other. Transfers can fundamentally change the bargain. The agreement should restrict voluntary transfers and address involuntary transfers.

Suggestions

Prohibit transfers without consent.

Allow limited estate planning transfers only if control remains with the original owner.

Require transferees to sign the agreement.

Distinguish economic rights from management rights.

Include rights of first refusal or first offer.

Address pledges and liens.

Address divorce, death, bankruptcy, and creditor claims.

Sample Clause: Transfer Restrictions

Prohibited Transfers. No Member may sell, assign, pledge, hypothecate, encumber, transfer, or otherwise dispose of all or any portion of its Membership Interest without the prior written consent of the other Member, except for Permitted Transfers expressly allowed under this Agreement.

Permitted Transfers. A Member may transfer all or part of its Membership Interest to a trust or entity established for estate planning purposes, provided that the transferring Member retains control, the transfer does not cause a default under any Company agreement, and the transferee agrees in writing to be bound by this Agreement.

Effect of Unauthorized Transfer. Any transfer made in violation of this Agreement shall be void and shall not confer upon the purported transferee any voting, consent, management, information, distribution, or economic rights.

16. Death, Disability, Divorce, Bankruptcy, and Creditor Events

The agreement should address what happens if an owner dies, becomes disabled, divorces, files bankruptcy, has assets seized, or becomes subject to creditor claims. Without clear provisions, the remaining owner may be forced to deal with heirs, spouses, trustees, receivers, or creditors.

Suggestions

Use buyout options for involuntary events.

Require life insurance if the business needs liquidity to fund a buyout.

Define permanent disability objectively.

Address whether heirs receive only economic rights.

Provide valuation and payment terms.

Protect the company from becoming involved in divorce or estate disputes.

Sample Clause: Involuntary Transfer Events

Involuntary Transfer Event. An Involuntary Transfer Event shall occur upon a Member death, permanent disability, bankruptcy, insolvency, assignment for benefit of creditors, divorce related transfer, levy, attachment, charging order, receivership, or other involuntary transfer or attempted transfer of all or any portion of such Member Membership Interest.

Purchase Option. Upon an Involuntary Transfer Event, the Company and, if the Company does not elect to purchase, the nonaffected Member shall have the option to purchase all or any portion of the affected Membership Interest at Fair Market Value determined under this Agreement.

Economic Rights Only. Unless admitted as a substitute Member with the written consent of the nonaffected Member, any transferee, heir, estate, spouse, creditor, trustee, receiver, or other successor shall hold only economic rights and shall have no voting, consent, management, or information rights except as required by applicable law.

17. Default, Cause, and Misconduct

The agreement should distinguish ordinary disagreement from misconduct. A deadlock is not necessarily a default. A bad business decision is not necessarily fraud. Theft, diversion, unauthorized transfers, repeated failure to perform duties, and intentional breach should have consequences.

Suggestions

Define cause carefully.

Provide notice and cure for curable defaults.

Do not allow cure for fraud or theft if cure would be inappropriate.

Use buyout discounts carefully.

Provide injunctive relief for misuse of confidential information or diversion of opportunities.

Sample Clause: Cause Event

Cause Event. A Cause Event shall mean any of the following by a Member: fraud; theft; embezzlement; misappropriation of Company funds or assets; intentional diversion of Company opportunities; felony conviction involving dishonesty; willful misconduct causing material harm to the Company; unauthorized transfer of a Membership Interest; material breach of this Agreement that remains uncured for fifteen business days after written notice if capable of cure; or repeated failure to perform material duties after written notice.

Remedies. Upon a Cause Event, the nondefaulting Member may seek injunctive relief, damages, accounting, removal of the defaulting Member from any management or officer role, and any other remedy available under this Agreement or applicable law.

Cause Buyout. In addition to other remedies, the nondefaulting Member shall have the option to purchase the defaulting Member Membership Interest at Fair Market Value, subject to a discount of ____ percent, unless such discount is prohibited by applicable law or determined by a court or arbitrator to be unenforceable.

18. Restrictive Covenants

Restrictive covenants are often important but must be tailored. Overbroad noncompetition provisions may be difficult to enforce and may be affected by changing law. Confidentiality, nonsolicitation, nondisparagement, and noncircumvention provisions are often more defensible if reasonably limited.

Suggestions

Use confidentiality broadly.

Use customer and employee nonsolicitation narrowly.

Limit noncompetition provisions by time, geography, and business scope if used at all.

Tie restrictions to legitimate business interests.

Distinguish restrictions during ownership from restrictions after exit.

Include modification language where appropriate.

Sample Clause: Confidentiality and Nonsolicitation

Confidentiality. Each Member shall keep confidential and shall not use or disclose any nonpublic information concerning the Company, including financial information, customer information, pricing, business plans, trade secrets, contracts, software, processes, and strategic information, except for Company purposes or as required by law.

Employee Nonsolicitation. During the term of this Agreement and for twelve months after ceasing to be a Member, no Member shall knowingly solicit for employment or engagement any employee or contractor of the Company with whom such Member had material contact through the Company, except through general solicitations not targeted at Company personnel.

Customer Nonsolicitation. During the term of this Agreement and for twelve months after ceasing to be a Member, no Member shall knowingly solicit any customer, client, or active prospect of the Company for a competing business if such Member had material contact with such customer, client, or prospect through the Company.

19. Tax Matters

Tax provisions are often ignored in simple 50:50 agreements. That is a mistake. If the entity is taxed as a partnership, tax allocations, tax distributions, partnership representative authority, audits, and capital accounts matter.

Suggestions

Identify the partnership representative for federal tax purposes.

Require consultation before tax elections.

Address tax distributions.

Address Section 754 elections where relevant.

Require delivery of K 1s by a target date.

Address imputed underpayment liability from tax audits.

Require cooperation after withdrawal or sale.

Sample Clause: Tax Matters

Tax Classification. Unless otherwise approved by both Members, the Company shall be treated as a partnership for federal and applicable state and local income tax purposes.

Partnership Representative. The Members shall appoint a Partnership Representative for federal tax purposes. The Partnership Representative shall not make material tax elections, extend statutes of limitation, settle tax proceedings, or bind the Company or any Member to material tax positions without the prior approval of both Members.

Tax Information. The Company shall use commercially reasonable efforts to provide each Member with Schedule K 1 and other information reasonably necessary for tax reporting no later than March 15 of each year.

Tax Distributions. The Company shall make tax distributions as provided in this Agreement, subject to available cash, lender restrictions, and reasonable reserves.

20. Dissolution and Winding Up

Every 50:50 agreement should state when the business ends and how it is wound up. Dissolution should not be left entirely to default law. The agreement should be clear about whether dissolution is available as an ordinary exit right or only after other procedures have failed.

Suggestions

List dissolution events.

Do not allow unilateral dissolution unless that is intended.

State who winds up.

Require sale of assets or distribution in kind.

Address final tax returns.

Address reserves.

Address payment order.

Address post dissolution authority.

Sample Clause: Dissolution

Dissolution Events. The Company shall dissolve only upon the first to occur of: approval of both Members; sale of all or substantially all Company assets; entry of a final nonappealable decree of judicial dissolution; or any other event requiring dissolution under applicable law that cannot be waived.

No Unilateral Dissolution. No Member may unilaterally dissolve, wind up, or terminate the Company except as expressly provided in this Agreement or required by nonwaivable applicable law.

Winding Up. Upon dissolution, the Members shall wind up the Company affairs in an orderly manner. The Company assets shall be applied first to creditors, including approved Member creditors, then to reasonable reserves, and then to the Members in accordance with their positive capital account balances or other distribution provisions set forth in this Agreement.

Continuing Authority. During winding up, the Members shall have authority only to preserve Company assets, satisfy liabilities, collect receivables, dispose of assets, complete existing obligations, file tax returns, and take actions reasonably necessary to wind up the Company.

21. Dispute Resolution, Forum, and Interim Remedies

A 50:50 agreement should include a dispute resolution clause, but it should not be generic. Owner disputes often require fast injunctive relief, accounting, document access, preservation of assets, and sometimes a receiver or temporary manager.

Suggestions

Choose court or arbitration deliberately.

For urgent remedies, preserve access to court.

Include venue and governing law.

Include fee shifting for enforcement.

Allow equitable relief for confidentiality breaches, misuse of funds, and diversion.

Consider expedited arbitration for valuation or buyout disputes.

Do not force all business decisions into arbitration if the company needs immediate action.

Sample Clause: Dispute Resolution

Governing Law. This Agreement shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the laws of the State of New York, without regard to conflict of law principles.

Forum. Except for disputes submitted to appraisal or expert determination under this Agreement, any action arising out of or relating to this Agreement shall be brought exclusively in the state or federal courts located in New York County, New York. Each Member submits to personal jurisdiction in such courts and waives any objection to venue or inconvenient forum.

Equitable Relief. Each Member acknowledges that unauthorized use of Company funds, disclosure of confidential information, diversion of Company opportunities, interference with Company operations, or violation of transfer restrictions may cause irreparable harm. The Company and each Member shall be entitled to seek temporary, preliminary, and permanent injunctive relief without the necessity of proving actual damages or posting bond, to the extent permitted by law.

Fees. The prevailing party in any action to enforce this Agreement shall be entitled to recover reasonable attorneys fees, expert fees, and costs.

22. Insurance and Risk Management

Insurance is often overlooked in 50:50 agreements. It can be essential for buyouts, liability protection, and business continuity.

Suggestions

Consider key person life insurance.

Consider disability buyout insurance.

Require general liability, property, cyber, professional liability, directors and officers, errors and omissions, employment practices liability, and workers compensation insurance as applicable.

State who controls policies.

Require notice before cancellation.

Use insurance to fund death buyouts where possible.

Sample Clause: Insurance

Insurance. The Company shall maintain insurance coverage in such amounts and types as are commercially reasonable for the Company business, including general liability, property, workers compensation, and such additional coverage as the Members approve. The Company may maintain key person, life, or disability insurance on either Member to fund buyout obligations or protect the Company from loss resulting from death or disability.

Policy Access. Each Member shall be entitled to receive copies of all insurance policies, renewal notices, claims correspondence, and cancellation notices.

23. Drafting for Different Types of 50:50 Businesses

Real Estate Holding Company

A real estate 50:50 LLC should focus on capital calls, refinancing, guarantees, leasing decisions, sale rights, major repairs, lender requirements, tax distributions, and exit rights. A sale process may be better than a shotgun if neither party should be forced into an operating relationship indefinitely.

Operating Business

An operating business should focus on management authority, compensation, employment duties, information rights, restrictive covenants, customer relationships, deadlock, and buyout triggers. A working owner and passive owner should not be treated the same for compensation purposes unless that is intentional.

Professional Practice

A professional practice should focus on licensure, client ownership, fee allocation, departures, malpractice insurance, nonsolicitation, records, and ethical restrictions. Buyout provisions must account for professional responsibility rules.

Family Business

A family business should focus on transfers, death, divorce, governance, employment of family members, distributions, and mechanisms to avoid personal disputes becoming company disputes.

Startup or Growth Company

A startup or growth company should focus on vesting, founder departures, dilution, financing, intellectual property ownership, restrictive covenants, board control, drag rights, and future investor requirements.

24. Practical Checklist

Checklist for a 50:50 Agreement

Entity purpose, management structure, officer authority, ordinary course authority, and reserved matters.

Annual budget, failure to approve budget, bank accounts, signature authority, books and records, monthly reporting, and tax matters.

Capital contributions, capital calls, failure to fund, member loans, distributions, compensation, member duties, and expense reimbursement.

Related party transactions, fiduciary duties, exculpation, confidentiality, business opportunities, competition, and solicitation.

Transfer restrictions, permitted transfers, rights of first refusal, death, disability, divorce, bankruptcy, creditor events, default, and cause events.

Buy sell rights, valuation, payment terms, deadlock, mediation, tie breakers, shotgun, sealed bid, appraisal buyout, sale process, dissolution, and winding up.

Governing law, forum, arbitration or litigation, equitable relief, fee shifting, insurance, amendments, and notices.

25. Conclusion

A 50:50 agreement should not be short just because the ownership split is simple. Equal ownership often requires more drafting, not less.

The most important recommendations are straightforward: define who runs the business day to day; list the decisions that require both owners approval; provide a clear process if the owners cannot agree; create a realistic exit mechanism; address funding defaults before they happen; separate ownership economics from employment compensation; require transparent books, records, and bank access; restrict affiliate transactions; plan for death, disability, divorce, bankruptcy, and creditor claims; and make dissolution a last resort unless the parties intentionally want easy exit rights.

The best 50:50 agreements are not pessimistic. They are practical. They recognize that sophisticated parties can trust each other and still need a written process for disagreement.
