409A Valuations, Fair Market Value, and Private Company Equity Grants

A practical guide for founders, boards, executives, and private companies issuing options or other equity compensation.

Startups & Venture · 2022

Key point: For a private company, the exercise price of stock options generally should be at least the fair market value of the underlying common stock on the grant date. A defensible 409A valuation helps support that fair market value and reduces the risk of adverse tax treatment.

Introduction

For private companies, equity compensation is often essential. Stock options can help recruit employees, retain executives, align incentives, conserve cash, and create upside for the people helping build the company. But private company equity grants come with a tax problem: unlike public company stock, there is no exchange-traded market price for the company’s common stock.

That is where Section 409A and fair market value become critical. A 409A valuation is a valuation process used to determine the fair market value of a private company’s common stock for purposes of granting stock options and certain other equity-based compensation.

The practical rule is simple: if a private company grants stock options, the option exercise price generally should be at least equal to the fair market value of the underlying common stock on the grant date. If the exercise price is below fair market value, the option may be treated as a discounted stock option and can create serious tax consequences under Section 409A.

What Section 409A Is

Section 409A of the Internal Revenue Code governs nonqualified deferred compensation. It applies broadly to arrangements that provide compensation in one year that may be paid in a later year. Although Section 409A is often discussed in the context of executive deferred compensation plans, it also matters for private company equity compensation.

Stock options, stock appreciation rights, deferred bonuses, severance arrangements, phantom equity, restricted stock units, and other compensation structures may raise Section 409A issues if they are not structured correctly.

The goal with ordinary private company stock options is usually to structure the option so that it is exempt from Section 409A, rather than merely compliant with Section 409A.

Why 409A Matters for Stock Options

Private company stock options are usually intended to be exempt from Section 409A. To fit within the common exemption for stock rights, the option generally must be granted with an exercise price that is at least equal to the fair market value of the underlying stock on the grant date and must satisfy other regulatory requirements.

If the exercise price is too low, the option can be treated as deferred compensation subject to Section 409A. That can cause the service provider, usually an employee, executive, director, advisor, or consultant, to recognize income earlier than expected and face additional taxes and penalties.

For a company, the risk is not only tax technical. A Section 409A problem can create employee relations issues, investor diligence issues, accounting complications, payroll and reporting problems, and closing problems in a financing or sale process.

What Fair Market Value Means

Fair market value generally means the price at which property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under compulsion and both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts. In the 409A context, the company is trying to determine the fair market value of its common stock as of a specific valuation date.

That date matters. A company does not need a theoretically perfect value for all time. It needs a reasonable, supportable fair market value determination as of the grant date or as of a valuation date that remains valid for the grant date.

Private company common stock may be worth less than the headline valuation implied by a preferred stock financing. Preferred stock may have liquidation preferences, dividends, anti-dilution protections, veto rights, redemption rights, information rights, and other economic and governance features that common stock does not have.

409A Value Is Not the Same as the Latest Financing Price

A common misunderstanding is that the latest preferred stock price automatically equals the fair market value of common stock. It usually does not.

If investors buy Series A preferred stock at $5.00 per share, the common stock fair market value might be lower because the preferred stock has superior rights. The valuation analysis must account for the company’s capital structure, the rights of each class of equity, company stage, volatility, risk, and expected exit outcomes.

This is why a company may have a high headline financing valuation while still granting options with an exercise price below the preferred stock price. That result is not automatically improper. The issue is whether the common stock valuation is reasonable and supportable.

What a 409A Valuation Actually Does

A 409A valuation typically determines the fair market value of the company’s common stock as of a specific date. The valuation provider usually reviews financial statements, capitalization records, financing history, projections, business model, market conditions, comparable companies, recent transactions, and the rights associated with each class of equity.

The valuation process often has three parts. First, the valuation provider estimates the value of the company as a whole. Second, the provider allocates enterprise value among preferred stock, common stock, options, warrants, and other securities. Third, the provider applies discounts or adjustments, where appropriate, to reflect lack of marketability, minority status, company stage, risk, and other valuation factors.

The result is a per-share fair market value for common stock. The board then uses that value when approving stock option grants.

Common Valuation Methods

Market Approach: Looks at comparable public companies or comparable transactions, often using revenue, EBITDA, or other industry multiples. This approach is more useful when the company has meaningful operating metrics or comparable businesses.

Income Approach: Estimates value based on projected future cash flows discounted back to present value. This is more useful for companies with meaningful forecasts, revenue visibility, or a path to profitability.

Asset Approach: Looks at net asset value. This may be relevant for holding companies, asset-heavy businesses, or very early-stage companies with limited operations.

Backsolve Method: Starts with the price paid by investors in a recent arm’s-length preferred stock financing and works backward to infer enterprise value and the value of common stock.

Option Pricing Method: Treats different equity classes as options on the company’s enterprise value. It is often used when outcomes are uncertain and the company has multiple classes of preferred stock.

Probability-Weighted Expected Return Method: Models potential outcomes, such as IPO, sale, dissolution, or continued private operation, assigns probabilities to those outcomes, and discounts expected values.

Current Value Method: Assumes a sale or liquidation on the valuation date and allocates value according to security rights and preferences. It is more common where an exit is imminent or the allocation is relatively straightforward.

Safe Harbor Valuations

One reason companies obtain independent 409A valuations is to seek safe harbor protection. Under the Treasury regulations, certain valuation methods can create a presumption that the valuation is reasonable, unless the IRS shows that the valuation was grossly unreasonable.

For private companies, the commonly discussed safe harbor approaches include an independent appraisal by a qualified valuation firm, a valuation based on a generally applicable repurchase formula in limited circumstances, and a valuation for certain illiquid startup companies performed by a qualified individual, subject to specific requirements.

In practice, venture-backed and growth companies usually use an independent third-party valuation firm. That approach is often the cleanest for tax, accounting, audit, investor, and transaction diligence purposes.

How Often a Company Needs a 409A Valuation

A common rule of thumb is that a private company obtains a new 409A valuation at least every 12 months. But a valuation may become stale earlier if there is a material event.

A company should not assume that a valuation remains usable simply because less than a year has passed. The real question is whether the prior valuation still reasonably reflects the company’s fair market value on the option grant date.

Material Events That May Require a New Valuation

A preferred stock financing or signed financing term sheet

A material change in revenue, bookings, gross margin, or projections

A major customer win or loss

A potential sale process or acquisition offer

A secondary sale of common stock

A major product launch, clinical milestone, regulatory event, or IP development

A material debt financing

A restructuring, down round, insolvency event, or significant market deterioration

Board Approval and Grant Timing

A 409A valuation is not self-executing. The board or authorized committee should formally approve the valuation and the option grants. The grant date, valuation date, board approval date, and exercise price should be internally consistent.

A clean process usually looks like this: the company obtains a current valuation report; the board reviews and approves the valuation; the board sets the fair market value of common stock; the board approves option grants with an exercise price at least equal to that fair market value; and the company promptly documents and communicates the grants.

FMV Is Not What the Founder Wants the Price to Be

Fair market value is not a compensation planning number. A company may want a low exercise price to make options more attractive, and employees may prefer lower exercise prices because they create more potential spread. But the company cannot simply choose a low number because it is convenient.

The valuation must reflect a reasonable determination of fair market value as of the grant date. The goal is not the lowest possible value. The goal is a defensible value.

Common 409A Mistakes

Granting options before getting a valuation: The company must support fair market value as of the grant date, not as of a later clean-up date.

Using an old valuation after a financing: A preferred stock financing is often a material event. Continuing to use a pre-financing valuation after closing can create risk.

Ignoring signed term sheets: A signed term sheet or credible financing offer may affect fair market value even before closing.

Backdating grants: Option grants should not be backdated. The grant date should match proper corporate approval.

Promising options before board approval: Offer letters should state that equity is subject to board approval and plan documents.

Treating advisor grants casually: Consultant and advisor grants can raise the same 409A concerns as employee grants.

Failing to update the cap table: Missing SAFEs, notes, warrants, option pool changes, or preferred rights can undermine the valuation.

Not coordinating documents: The equity plan, option agreement, board consent, valuation report, and cap table should align.

What Happens If Options Are Granted Below FMV

If options are granted with an exercise price below fair market value and do not otherwise comply with Section 409A, the tax consequences can be severe for the option holder. Potential consequences can include immediate income inclusion when the option vests, a 20% additional federal tax, interest-type charges, possible state tax consequences, and withholding or reporting issues.

The company may also face payroll reporting, withholding, financial statement, and diligence consequences. In a financing or acquisition, investors or buyers may require corrective action, indemnities, escrows, special disclosures, or purchase price adjustments.

ISOs, NSOs, RSUs, Phantom Equity, and Profits Interests

Both incentive stock options and nonqualified stock options should be granted at no less than fair market value if they are intended to avoid discounted-option problems. ISO status does not eliminate the need to determine fair market value.

Restricted stock units and phantom equity often implicate Section 409A because they are deferred compensation unless structured to fit an exemption or comply with payment timing rules. Stock appreciation rights can be exempt if structured properly, including having a base price at least equal to fair market value on the grant date.

For LLCs taxed as partnerships, companies often use profits interests rather than stock options. Profits interests raise different tax issues and require separate analysis involving partnership tax rules, capital accounts, liquidation value, vesting, repurchase rights, and possible 83(b) elections.

What Companies Should Put in Place

Maintain an updated capitalization table

Adopt a board-approved equity incentive plan

Obtain a current 409A valuation before granting options

Refresh the valuation at least annually and after material events

Approve grants at the board or committee level

Set exercise prices at or above fair market value

Avoid promising fixed exercise prices in offer letters

Use consistent grant documentation

Track vesting, exercises, terminations, and repurchases

Coordinate counsel, accountants, valuation providers, and equity administration platforms

Keep the valuation report, board approvals, option agreements, and grant notices in the company records

Sample Board Approval Language

Approval of Fair Market Value. The Board reviewed the valuation report prepared by __________ dated __________, determining the fair market value of the Company’s common stock as of __________. After discussion and consideration of the report, the Company’s financial condition, capitalization, recent developments, and other relevant factors, the Board determined that the fair market value of the Company’s common stock is $__________ per share as of the date of this approval.

Approval of Option Grants. The Board approved the grant of stock options to the individuals and in the amounts set forth on Exhibit A, with an exercise price of $__________ per share, which is not less than the fair market value of the Company’s common stock as determined by the Board. The options shall be granted under the Company’s equity incentive plan and shall be subject to the applicable form of option agreement and grant notice.

Authorization. The officers of the Company are authorized and directed to take all actions necessary or advisable to implement the foregoing resolutions, including preparing, executing, and delivering grant documents and updating the Company’s capitalization records.

Sample Offer Letter Language

Equity Grant. Subject to approval by the Company’s Board of Directors or an authorized committee, you will be eligible to receive an option to purchase __________ shares of the Company’s common stock under the Company’s equity incentive plan. The exercise price will be determined by the Board or authorized committee and will be no less than the fair market value of the Company’s common stock on the date of grant. Any equity award will be subject to the terms of the applicable plan, grant notice, and award agreement.

Sample 409A Policy Language

409A Valuation and Grant Policy. The Company shall not grant stock options or stock appreciation rights unless the Board of Directors or an authorized committee has determined the fair market value of the underlying common stock as of the grant date. The Company shall obtain an independent valuation of its common stock at least annually and more frequently after any material event that may affect fair market value, including equity financings, material changes in financial performance, significant commercial developments, acquisition proposals, or other material corporate events. All option exercise prices shall be set at not less than the fair market value determined in accordance with this policy.

Practical Takeaways

A 409A valuation is not just a compliance formality. It is the foundation for private company stock option pricing.

For companies, the goal is to grant equity in a way that is attractive to employees and defensible to the IRS, investors, auditors, and future buyers. For employees, the goal is to receive options that do not carry hidden tax problems. For boards, the goal is to create a record showing that fair market value was determined reasonably and contemporaneously.

Do not grant options without a current fair market value determination.

Do not use an old valuation after a material event.

Do not treat preferred stock price and common stock value as the same thing.

Do not promise exercise prices before board approval.

Do document the board’s valuation determination.

Do refresh the valuation regularly.

Do coordinate legal, tax, accounting, valuation, and cap table records.
