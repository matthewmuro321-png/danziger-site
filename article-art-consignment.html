The consignment agreement

Consignment is how most art is sold, and the moment of greatest risk to the owner. The agreement, and New York law, decide who is protected when the dealer holds the work, and the money.

Art Law · 2018

A consignment is not a sale. When an owner consigns a work to a dealer or gallery, title stays with the owner, the dealer is authorized to sell it on the owner's behalf, and the dealer remits the proceeds less a commission. It is the ordinary machinery of the art trade, repeated thousands of times, and it places the owner in a position of unusual exposure that the routine of it tends to obscure. For a period, and then for the proceeds of a sale, the dealer is holding what belongs to someone else. The agreement that governs that period, and the law that backs it, determine whether the owner is protected if the dealer fails.

The risk that defines consignment

The danger is concrete and recurring. A dealer who becomes insolvent, or who simply misbehaves, may have the consignor's work on its walls and the consignor's money in its accounts at the moment things go wrong. Absent protection, that work and those proceeds can be swept up by the dealer's own creditors, leaving the consignor as one more unsecured claimant chasing property that was always theirs. The art trade's history is dotted with galleries that collapsed owing artists and collectors for works long since sold. The protections below exist precisely because this is not a hypothetical risk.

New York's statutory trust

New York gives consignors one of the strongest protections in the country. Under the State's Arts and Cultural Affairs Law, a work delivered to a dealer on consignment, together with the proceeds of its sale, is treated as trust property held for the benefit of the consignor. The dealer is the consignor's fiduciary, the work and the money are not the dealer's to use or to pledge, and they are insulated from the claims of the dealer's creditors. The protection is strongest of all for artists consigning their own work, for whom the statutory safeguards cannot be waived, a deliberate response to the imbalance of power between an artist and a gallery. For artists in particular, New York is a favorable place to consign.

The Uniform Commercial Code dimension

The statutory trust is powerful, but a careful consignor adds a second layer. A consignment can also be a consignment under Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code, and where it is, the consignor's interest can be defeated by the dealer's secured creditors, or by certain buyers, unless the consignor takes the steps the Code requires. Filing a financing statement, and in some cases giving notice to the dealer's existing secured creditors, perfects and protects the consignor's interest against those competing claims. The New York trust and the Code filing are complementary rather than redundant, and for a non-artist consignor, or where works will sit with a dealer over time, the filing is the belt to the statute's suspenders.

The protection to claim

New York's trust arises by operation of law, but the consignor who pairs it with a clear written agreement and, where appropriate, a UCC filing is the one whose work and proceeds are genuinely secure if a dealer fails. Relying on the statute alone, without the paper, leaves protection on the table.

What the agreement must say

A sound consignment agreement converts the trust the arrangement demands into terms the parties can enforce. At its core it identifies the work and confirms that title remains with the consignor, sets the term of the consignment, and fixes the economics, meaning the net or minimum price the consignor must receive and the dealer's commission. It defines and limits the dealer's authority, including any discretion to discount and the buyers to whom the work may be sold, so that the dealer cannot commit the consignor to a price or a purchaser the consignor would refuse.

It must also protect the work while the dealer holds it and the money once the work sells. That means requiring insurance and allocating the risk of loss or damage, specifying care and storage, and, after a sale, requiring the dealer to remit the proceeds promptly and to keep them segregated rather than commingled with the dealer's own funds. It should give the consignor rights to regular reporting, to inspect the work, and to recall it, and it should allocate the expenses of shipping, framing, photography, and marketing. Rounding it out are the warranties, any exclusivity or territory, the treatment of the dealer's insolvency, the condition in which the work must be returned, and the terms of termination.

Terms a consignment agreement should carry

01

Title and term. Confirmation that title stays with the consignor, and the duration of the consignment.

02

Price and commission. The net or minimum price to the consignor and the dealer's commission.

03

Dealer authority. Limits on discounting and on the buyers to whom the work may be sold.

04

Insurance and risk of loss. Coverage and allocation of risk while the work is with the dealer.

05

Care and storage. How and where the work is kept.

06

Remittance and segregation. Prompt payment of proceeds, held separately, not commingled.

07

Reporting, inspection, recall. The consignor's visibility and control over the work.

08

Expenses, warranties, insolvency, return. Who pays for what, and what happens if the dealer fails.

Artists and collectors are not in the same position

The two kinds of consignor must be distinguished, because the law distinguishes them. An artist consigning original work to a gallery enjoys the strongest statutory protection, including safeguards that cannot be bargained away, and should still insist on a clear agreement that fixes the price, the reporting, and the remittance terms. A collector or estate consigning to a dealer relies more heavily on the agreement and on a UCC filing to secure its position, and has more to lose by treating the consignment casually. In both cases the lesson is the same. Art is placed with dealers on relationships and trust, and that is unlikely to change, but the consignor who adds a clear agreement and the available statutory and Code protections is the one who keeps the work, and the proceeds, when trust is not enough.
