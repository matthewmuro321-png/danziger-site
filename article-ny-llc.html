Forming and maintaining a New York LLC

New York makes an LLC easy to create and full of small obligations that catch owners off guard, from a costly publication rule to a brand-new transparency law whose reach just narrowed.

Business Formation · 2026

Forming a limited liability company in New York takes a single filing and a couple of hundred dollars. Keeping it in good standing takes considerably more attention, because New York layers on a set of requirements that few owners anticipate and several that are easy to miss. There is an operating agreement the statute actually requires, a publication obligation that can cost more than the formation itself, an assumed-name filing for anyone doing business under a different name, recurring filings to stay current, and, as of 2026, a new beneficial-ownership transparency law whose practical scope shifted at the last moment. Each is manageable. None should be a surprise.

Formation and the name

An LLC comes into existence when its Articles of Organization are filed with the New York Department of State, currently for a $200 fee. The articles are short, identifying the company, the county of its office, and the Secretary of State as the agent on whom process may be served. The company's name must include a signal of its form, such as "LLC" or "Limited Liability Company," must be distinguishable from names already on file, and cannot use certain restricted words without approval. That is the easy part, and it is where many owners assume the obligations end. They do not.

The operating agreement New York actually requires

New York is one of the few states that, by statute, requires an LLC to have a written operating agreement. The members must adopt it before, at the time of, or within ninety days after filing the articles. It is an internal document, not filed with the state, and there is no direct penalty for failing to have one, which leads many owners to skip it. That is a mistake. Without an operating agreement, the default provisions of the LLC Law govern the company's management, economics, and the members' relationships, and those defaults are rarely what the owners would have chosen. For a company with more than one member especially, the operating agreement is where the most important decisions about control, money, and exit are made, and it is far cheaper to make them at the outset than to litigate them later.

The publication requirement, and why it surprises people

This is the obligation that catches owners off guard. Within 120 days of formation, a New York LLC must publish a notice of its formation once a week for six successive weeks in two newspapers, one daily and one weekly, designated by the county clerk of the county where the LLC's office is located. After publication, the company files a Certificate of Publication, with the printers' affidavits, with the Department of State. The same requirement applies to out-of-state LLCs that register to do business in New York.

Two things make this requirement notable. The first is cost. Because the newspapers are designated by the county clerk and the rates are set by the publishers, the expense varies widely by county and can be substantial, with New York City counties, and Manhattan in particular, among the most expensive, sometimes costing more than the formation itself. The second is the consequence of ignoring it. An LLC that fails to publish has its authority to carry on business in New York suspended. That sounds dire, and owners should not ignore it, but it is narrower than it appears: the suspension does not void the company's contracts, does not prevent it from defending a lawsuit, and does not disturb the members' limited liability. It is also curable, by completing the publication and filing the certificate even late, at which point the authority is restored.

Practice note

Budget for publication when you form the company, and calendar the 120-day deadline. The cost is unavoidable, but the suspension is, and curing it late after a problem has surfaced is more expensive and more disruptive than simply doing it on time.

Doing business under another name: assumed names

An LLC that operates under any name other than its exact legal name must file a Certificate of Assumed Name. For an LLC, this filing goes to the Department of State, not to the county clerk, which is a point of confusion because individuals and general partnerships file their assumed-name certificates at the county level instead. The filing carries a base fee plus an additional fee for each county in which the LLC conducts business under the assumed name, and the assumed name itself must comply with the same naming rules that apply to the company's true name. Any company that markets or contracts under a brand different from the name on its articles needs this filing, and operating under an unregistered assumed name can create problems precisely when the company most needs its paperwork to be clean.

Staying in good standing

Two recurring obligations keep an LLC current. New York requires a Biennial Statement, filed every two years with the Department of State for a small fee, updating the address to which the Secretary of State forwards any legal process. Separately, an LLC with New York-source income owes an annual filing fee to the State's tax department, on a sliding scale that currently runs from $25 to $4,500 depending on the company's New York-source gross income, reported on its own short form. Neither is burdensome, but both are easy to forget, and lapsing on them undermines the good standing a company needs to finance, contract, and transact.

What's new: the LLC Transparency Act

The significant recent development is the New York LLC Transparency Act, enacted in 2023, amended in 2024, and effective January 1, 2026. Modeled on the federal Corporate Transparency Act, it was designed to require LLCs to disclose their beneficial owners, the individuals who own or control them, to the Department of State, with the information held in a confidential database rather than published publicly.

Its practical reach, however, narrowed sharply right before it took effect, and this is the point owners most need to understand today. Because the New York law defines who must report by reference to the federal Corporate Transparency Act, and because the federal government in March 2025 narrowed that definition to exclude companies formed in the United States, New York's Department of State confirmed at the end of 2025 that the Act currently applies only to LLCs formed outside the United States that are authorized to do business in New York. As things stand, US-formed LLCs, including ordinary New York LLCs, are not required to file beneficial-ownership reports under the Act. Non-US LLCs that are subject to it must report, with those authorized before 2026 facing a deadline at the end of 2026 and those authorized later reporting within thirty days, followed by annual updates.

Watch this one

This is the most fluid area in New York LLC compliance. US-formed LLCs are exempt today only because New York's law tracks the current, narrowed federal definition. If the federal rules change again, or if New York amends its statute to reach domestic LLCs directly, the exemption could disappear. Owners should treat the current position as provisional and monitor it.

The New York LLC filings, at a glance

01

Articles of Organization. Filed with the Department of State to form the company.

02

Operating agreement. Required by statute within 90 days; internal, not filed with the state.

03

Publication and Certificate of Publication. Two newspapers for six weeks within 120 days, then file the certificate.

04

Certificate of Assumed Name. Filed with the Department of State if the company uses any other name.

05

Biennial Statement. Every two years, updating the address for service of process.

06

Annual filing fee. Paid to the tax department on a sliding scale by New York-source income.

07

Beneficial-ownership report. Under the Transparency Act, currently required only of non-US LLCs.
