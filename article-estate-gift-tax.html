Estate and gift tax in 2026, and how families give

The federal exemption is now permanently at a historic high. New York's far lower tax, and its cliff, still catch families off guard. And once the tax is handled, the question becomes how to give.

Family Office and Private Wealth · 2026

For a family of substantial means, the transfer tax system is the quiet backdrop to nearly every planning decision, and for 2026 the picture has changed in a way worth understanding. The federal exemption, long expected to be cut roughly in half at the end of 2025, was instead made permanent at a historic high. That removes a deadline that had driven a great deal of urgent planning, and it shifts the focus to questions that were always there underneath: state estate tax, income tax basis, and the non-tax goals of governance and legacy. For New York families in particular, the federal good news does not tell the whole story, because New York imposes its own, much harsher tax with a trap built into it.

The federal system, in brief

The federal government taxes the transfer of wealth in three linked ways: an estate tax on what passes at death, a gift tax on lifetime gifts, and a generation-skipping transfer tax on transfers that skip a generation. These share a single lifetime exemption and a top rate of 40 percent. Layered on top is the annual exclusion, which lets a person give a set amount to any number of recipients each year without using any exemption, alongside unlimited direct payments of tuition and medical expenses, an unlimited marital deduction for transfers to a citizen spouse, and an unlimited charitable deduction. Portability allows a surviving spouse to use a deceased spouse's unused exemption, if the election is made.

For 2026, the numbers are these. The unified exemption is $15 million per person, or $30 million for a married couple, made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and indexed for inflation going forward. The annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient. The practical effect is that the large majority of families now fall comfortably below the federal threshold, and the planning question is no longer how to beat a sunset but how to use a durable, generous exemption well, with more attention than before to preserving the income tax basis step-up at death and to goals that have nothing to do with tax.

New York's separate, and harsher, tax

New York imposes its own estate tax, entirely separate from the federal one and far less forgiving. For 2026 the New York exemption is $7,350,000 per person, less than half the federal figure, with a top rate of 16 percent. New York offers no portability between spouses, so a couple must plan deliberately to use both exemptions rather than relying on the survivor to inherit the first spouse's unused amount.

Then there is the cliff, which is unlike anything in the federal system. Most taxes apply only to the amount above an exemption. New York instead phases the exemption out as the estate approaches 105 percent of it, and at that point the exemption vanishes entirely, so the whole estate is taxed from the first dollar. In 2026 the edge of the cliff is $7,717,500. The consequence is jarring: an estate of $7.35 million may owe no New York tax at all, while an estate only modestly larger, above the cliff, can owe tax on its entire value, a difference that can cost a family hundreds of thousands of dollars over a small change in size.

The New York trap, and a lever

A family relying on the $15 million federal exemption can overlook New York entirely and walk straight off the cliff. Because New York has no gift tax, lifetime gifting can bring an estate under the threshold, but a three-year clawback pulls gifts made within three years of death back into the estate, so the gifting must be done well in advance. A charitable bequest sized to drop the estate just under the cliff is another common fix.

The levers, in short

The tools for managing transfer tax are familiar, and with a permanent high exemption they are deployed with a longer horizon and less urgency than during the sunset years. Lifetime gifting, using both the annual exclusion and the lifetime exemption, removes assets and their future appreciation from the estate. Trusts of various kinds, including grantor retained annuity trusts, dynasty and generation-skipping trusts, and spousal lifetime access trusts, move wealth efficiently while retaining structure and control. Valuation planning, charitable giving, and, for those willing, a change of domicile away from New York all have their place. With the federal exemption no longer the binding constraint for most families, state estate tax, basis planning, and governance often drive the plan.

How families give: foundation or donor-advised fund

For families who give at scale, two structures dominate, and choosing between them is one of the more consequential decisions a philanthropic family makes. Both deliver an income tax deduction and a vehicle for organized giving. They differ fundamentally on a single axis: control versus simplicity.

The private foundation is the vehicle of control and legacy. The family owns and directs a separate entity, decides its grants and its investments, can give in ways a donor-advised fund cannot, including scholarships and grants beyond ordinary public charities, and can carry the family's name across generations. That control comes at a price: meaningful setup and administrative cost, a requirement to distribute roughly five percent of assets each year, a modest excise tax on investment income, a strict regime of rules against self-dealing and other prohibited acts, lower charitable deduction limits, and public disclosure of the foundation's activities. A foundation is for families who want to build an institution and will accept the obligations that come with one.

The donor-advised fund is the vehicle of simplicity and efficiency. It is an account at a sponsoring charity that the donor funds, takes an immediate deduction for, and then recommends grants from over time. It is inexpensive and easy to establish, carries higher deduction limits, readily accepts appreciated assets, and can be operated anonymously, with no foundation-style payout requirement. The trade is control: the donor recommends rather than directs, grants are limited to public charities, and the family cannot be compensated or make the kinds of grants a foundation can. For many families, the donor-advised fund delivers most of what they want with a fraction of the burden.

The two are not mutually exclusive, and sophisticated families often use both, a foundation for the control, the legacy, and the giving a foundation uniquely allows, and a donor-advised fund alongside it for flexibility, efficiency, and the occasional anonymous gift. The right structure, or the right pairing, follows from what the family is actually trying to accomplish.
